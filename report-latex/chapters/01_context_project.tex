\section{Presentation of the Project}

\subsection{Project Context}
Manual queue supervision is often subjective, hard to scale, and dependent on continuous attention. In many settings, teams react only after congestion appears instead of anticipating it with measurable indicators.

This project addresses that gap by turning camera streams into decision-oriented telemetry. The target output is not limited to occupancy counts, but includes people in zone, arrival rate, service rate, expected wait time, queue stability state, and alert states that can be consumed by monitoring and automation layers.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Current operational practices and basic counting tools still have recurring limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item they provide limited anticipation of congestion,
    \item they rarely estimate waiting time explicitly,
    \item they provide weak visibility into queue stability during sudden demand spikes,
    \item they are often disconnected from automated response workflows.
\end{itemize}

As a result, interventions such as staff reallocation, lane opening, or escalation can be delayed or triggered on incomplete signals. The problem is therefore both analytical and integration-related: it is not enough to produce robust indicators; they also need to be delivered through actionable channels.

\subsection{Study of Existing Approaches}
Several computer-vision frameworks provide the basic building blocks for person detection and tracking. In parallel, queueing theory gives a formal way to describe service-system behavior and relate arrival rate, service rate, and expected waiting time \cite{gross_queueing}.

In practice, existing commercial and open-source tools cover only parts of the queue-management problem. They fall into three broad families: queue management platforms, sensor-based analytics, and vision-based monitoring frameworks.

\subsubsection{Queue Management Platforms}
Platforms such as Waitwhile \cite{waitwhile2026} and Qminder \cite{qminder2026} focus on active queue management. They let customers join virtual queues through kiosks, QR codes, or web links, and they notify staff through SMS or dashboards. Their strength is customer-facing experience: reduced perceived waiting, appointment scheduling, and service routing. However, they require explicit customer interaction (check-in) and do not observe the physical queue directly. They cannot estimate wait time from the scene itself, and they do not detect congestion unless a customer has already registered.

\subsubsection{Sensor-Based Analytics}
RetailNext \cite{retailnext2026} and Xovis \cite{xovis2026} represent the sensor-based analytics family. RetailNext uses ceiling-mounted AI sensors to track foot traffic, measure conversion rates, and optimize staffing in retail environments. Xovis uses 3D optical sensors for precise people flow and queue length measurement, particularly in airports and large retail spaces. Both provide high-accuracy occupancy and dwell-time data, but they require proprietary hardware installations and are primarily designed for large-scale enterprise deployments. They observe the queue passively but do not typically integrate with external automation workflows or provide open APIs for custom alert routing.

\subsubsection{Vision-Based Monitoring Frameworks}
Open-source tools built on YOLO detection and tracking, such as those provided by Ultralytics \cite{ultralytics_yolo2026} and Roboflow \cite{roboflow_queue}, offer the computer-vision building blocks needed for queue monitoring: person detection, tracking, and zone-based counting. These frameworks are flexible and can be adapted to specific use cases. However, they typically stop at detection and counting. They do not include queue-theoretic estimation of wait time, stability classification, or integration with notification workflows. The gap between raw detection output and actionable operational metrics is left to the developer.

The proposed solution fills the gap between these families by combining open-source vision-based detection with queue-theoretic analytics, a web monitoring dashboard, and automated alert routing, all in one modular pipeline that does not require proprietary hardware or explicit customer interaction.

\subsection{Proposed Solution}
The system combines perception, analytics, and integration in one modular pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item person detection and multi-object tracking from camera feeds,
    \item polygon-based queue-zone filtering,
    \item estimation of $\lambda$, $\mu$, and expected wait time,
    \item stable/unstable queue-state interpretation for safe operational decisions,
    \item real-time web supervision through tiered live transport (WebRTC primary, MJPEG fallback),
    \item automation through n8n webhooks and Telegram alerts.
\end{enumerate}

This architecture converts visual observations into operational actions while keeping each layer independently testable. Detection and tracking modules can evolve without rewriting queue formulas, and the web and automation channels can evolve without changing the core metric contract.

\subsection{Objectives of the Project}
The project objectives are to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item detect and track people in real time,
    \item count people in configurable queue zones,
    \item estimate arrival and service rates,
    \item compute expected wait time,
    \item classify queue stability for operational reliability,
    \item expose metrics through web interfaces and automation workflows.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Project Scope and Boundaries}
The implementation scope covers backend perception and analytics modules, API and runtime integration, web monitoring, and external workflow automation. The report focuses on this web-first operational architecture.

All implementation details and validation narratives are presented through the backend, web, and automation tracks to keep the scope consistent.

\subsection{Target Audience}
The system is intended for three stakeholder groups with complementary goals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Administrators}: manage manager accounts and access governance.
    \item \textbf{Managers}: configure operational settings, monitor live queue status, react to alerts, and use indicators for staffing and service-quality planning.
    \item \textbf{End users}: benefit indirectly from reduced waiting times and smoother service flow.
\end{itemize}

This separation is important because each group consumes different levels of abstraction, from per-feed live events to aggregated performance trends.

\section{Project Management Methodology}

\subsection{Choice of Methodology: Agile}
An Agile approach was selected to deliver functional increments quickly while reducing integration risks early. The project combines multiple technical domains, including computer vision, queue analytics, backend APIs, frontend workflows, and automation, and each one has uncertainties that are difficult to resolve in a single delivery cycle.

Iterative delivery made it possible to validate contracts progressively: first perception outputs, then analytical reliability, then API/runtime integration, then web usability, and finally workflow automation and hardening.

Development followed sprint-based Scrum planning with iterative validation. For report readability and implementation traceability, the work is presented as four consolidated sprint chapters that are grouped into three logical modules.
