\section{Presentation of the Project}

\subsection{Project Context}
Manual queue supervision is often subjective, hard to scale, and dependent on continuous attention. In many settings, teams react only after congestion appears instead of anticipating it with measurable indicators.

This project addresses that gap by turning camera streams into decision-oriented telemetry. The target output includes people in zone, arrival rate, service rate, adaptive wait-time estimates, and alert states for monitoring and automation layers.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Current operational practices and basic counting tools still have recurring limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item they provide limited anticipation of congestion,
    \item they rarely estimate waiting time explicitly,
    \item they provide weak visibility into queue stability during sudden demand spikes,
    \item they are often disconnected from automated response workflows.
\end{itemize}

As a result, interventions such as staff reallocation, lane opening, or escalation can be delayed or triggered on incomplete signals. The problem is both analytical and integration-related, and Table~\ref{tab:painpoint_to_requirement} maps these pain points to system-level requirements.

\subsection{Study of Existing Approaches}
Several computer-vision frameworks provide the basic building blocks for person detection and tracking. In parallel, queueing theory gives a formal way to describe service-system behavior and relate arrival rate, service rate, and expected waiting time \cite{gross_queueing}.

In practice, existing commercial and open-source tools cover only parts of the queue-management problem. They fall into three broad families: queue management platforms, sensor-based analytics, and vision-based monitoring frameworks.

\subsubsection{Queue Management Platforms}
Platforms such as Waitwhile \cite{waitwhile2026} and Qminder \cite{qminder2026} focus on active queue management. They let customers join virtual queues through kiosks, QR codes, or web links, and they notify staff through SMS or dashboards. Their strength is customer-facing experience: reduced perceived waiting, appointment scheduling, and service routing. However, they require explicit customer interaction (check-in) and do not observe the physical queue directly. They cannot estimate wait time from the scene itself, and they do not detect congestion unless a customer has already registered.

\subsubsection{Sensor-Based Analytics}
RetailNext \cite{retailnext2026} and Xovis \cite{xovis2026} represent the sensor-based analytics family. RetailNext uses ceiling-mounted AI sensors to track foot traffic, measure conversion rates, and optimize staffing in retail environments. Xovis uses 3D optical sensors for precise people flow and queue length measurement, particularly in airports and large retail spaces. Both provide high-accuracy occupancy and dwell-time data, but they require proprietary hardware installations and are primarily designed for large-scale enterprise deployments. They observe the queue passively but do not typically integrate with external automation workflows or provide open APIs for custom alert routing.

\subsubsection{Vision-Based Monitoring Frameworks}
Open-source tools built on YOLO detection and tracking, such as those provided by Ultralytics \cite{ultralytics_docs} and Roboflow \cite{roboflow_queue}, offer the computer-vision building blocks needed for queue monitoring: person detection, tracking, and zone-based counting. These frameworks are flexible and can be adapted to specific use cases. However, they typically stop at detection and counting. They do not include queue-theoretic estimation of wait time, stability classification, or integration with notification workflows. The gap between raw detection output and actionable operational metrics is left to the developer.

\subsection{Project Scope, Boundaries, and Stakeholders}
The implementation scope addresses three distinct but interdependent technical domains: backend perception and analytics modules, API and runtime integration, and web-based operational interfaces. The report presentation mirrors this structure to maintain alignment between architectural decisions and validation activities.

The system is designed for three stakeholder groups with complementary operational responsibilities. Administrators manage account governance and access controls. Managers configure operational settings, monitor live queue status, respond to alerts, and use performance indicators for staffing and service-quality decisions. End users benefit indirectly through reduced waiting times and improved service flow. This separation reflects both organizational hierarchy and the varying levels of technical abstraction required by each stakeholder group, from per-feed real-time events to aggregated performance trends.

\section{Project Management Methodology}

\subsection{Choice of Methodology: Agile}
An Agile approach was selected to deliver functional increments quickly while reducing integration risks early. The project combines multiple technical domains, including computer vision, queue analytics, backend APIs, frontend workflows, and automation, and each one has uncertainties that are difficult to resolve in a single delivery cycle.

Iterative delivery made it possible to validate contracts progressively: first perception outputs, then analytical reliability, then API/runtime integration, then web usability, and finally workflow automation and hardening.

\subsection{SCRUM}
Development followed a sprint-based Scrum methodology with iterative validation cycles. Each sprint focused on a coherent architectural or functional segment, progressing from foundational perception and analytics components through web interface development and finally to automation and system hardening.
